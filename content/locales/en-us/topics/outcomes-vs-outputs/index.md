# Outcomes vs Outputs

An output is the direct, countable product of an activity — it exists the moment delivery happens,
regardless of what effect it has. An outcome is the change that follows for the people, place, or
system involved. "500 people attended a job-search workshop" is an output: it is true even if none
of them find work. "500 people's employment prospects improved" is an outcome claim, and it requires
evidence of change, not just evidence of attendance — the confusion that produces more misleading
grant reports than almost any other measurement mistake in the sector.

## Why it matters

HM Treasury's Magenta Book and funders such as the National Lottery Community Fund both require
outcome reporting specifically because outputs are what programs report by default: they are
cheap to count, always available, and always look positive. An output count can literally never go
down as a result of the program failing — more sessions delivered is always "more", whereas an
outcome can reveal a program is not working. The National Audit Office has repeatedly criticized
government programs for reporting activity levels as if they were evidence of success; a software
system that only makes outputs easy to report reinforces this by default, because outputs require
no follow-up data collection and outcomes do.

## The math

There is no formula, but there is a reliable test to classify a metric:

```
Output test:  is it countable at the point of delivery, true even if the recipient is unaffected?
Outcome test: does it require a before/after or with/without comparison to be meaningful?

If a number can be true with zero benefit to anyone, it is an output.
```

This sits inside the wider [logic model](../logic-model/) chain and depends on the outcome links
defined in a [theory of change](../theory-of-change/); turning an outcome into money uses the
methods in [social return on investment](../social-return-on-investment/).

## Worked example

**Local authority (employment support)**: output — 500 people attended job-search workshops.
Outcome — at 12-month follow-up, 140 of those 500 (28%) are in sustained employment (6+ months). A
comparison group with similar characteristics but no program access has a 15% baseline
employment rate over the same period. Net outcome uplift: 28% − 15% = 13 percentage points, so an
estimated 500 × 0.13 = 65 additional people are in work who would not otherwise have been — the
attributable outcome, distinct from either the 500 attendance figure or the 140 raw employment
count.

**Charity (literacy charity)**: output — 1,200 reading sessions delivered to 300 children. Outcome —
average reading age improved by 8 months over a 6-month period, against an expected 6-month natural
progression baseline of 6 months. Net outcome gain: 8 − 6 = 2 months' additional reading age
improvement per child attributable to the program, not the full 8-month figure.

## Software engineering connection

Event logs and transactional systems instrument outputs almost automatically — page views,
sessions, tickets closed, appointments booked — because they are generated by the system doing its
job. Outcomes require a data model that captures the same individual at a later point in time
against a baseline or comparison, which has to be designed in deliberately: follow-up surveys,
linked administrative records, or a comparison cohort. A reporting tool that only supports the
former will quietly steer an organization toward output-only reporting no matter what the funder
asked for. See [logic model](../logic-model/) for where outcomes sit in the accountability chain,
[cost-per-outcome](../cost-per-outcome/) for turning this distinction into a unit-cost metric, and
[public sector KPIs](../public-sector-kpis/) for the wider pattern of metric selection.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting outputs as if they were outcomes.** "500 people attended" implies benefit without
  demonstrating it; label attendance as an output explicitly.
- **No baseline or comparison group.** An outcome figure with no counterfactual — see
  [counterfactual analysis](../counterfactual-analysis/) — cannot separate program effect from
  what would have happened anyway.
- **Optimizing for the funded metric.** When funding is tied to output volume, delivery teams
  rationally maximize attendance over durable change, a Goodhart's law failure mode.
- **Outcome washing.** Relabeling an output metric with outcome-sounding language ("engagement
  outcomes: 500 attendees") without any follow-up measurement behind it.
